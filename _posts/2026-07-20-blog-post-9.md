### *"The greatest breakthroughs often begin not by fighting complexity, but by discovering what remains unchanged within it."*


Some of the most meaningful discoveries in life begin not with an answer but with a question that quietly refuses to disappear. For me, that question emerged during the summer of 2026 while I was attending **Ordinary Differential Equations** at the **New Mexico Institute of Mining and Technology**. Like many students, I entered the course expecting to learn techniques for solving differential equations. Every lecture introduced another elegant method developed over centuries by mathematicians whose ideas continue to shape modern science and engineering. Among these methods, one in particular captured my attention: the **characteristic equation**. At first, I accepted it simply because it worked. We assumed that the solution had the form of an exponential function, substituted it into the differential equation, derived an algebraic equation, solved for its roots, and then constructed the complete solution. The procedure was systematic, elegant, and remarkably efficient. Yet every time I wrote the expression $(y=e^{rx})$, I found myself asking a simple question that none of the calculations seemed to answer: **Why does this particular function work?**

Most mathematics textbooks present the characteristic equation as a finished product. They begin by stating, almost without explanation, "Assume that the solution has the form $(y=e^{rx})$." The assumption is then justified by its success, and after solving several examples it gradually becomes another familiar tool in the student's mathematical toolbox. However, I found it difficult to accept such an important idea without understanding where it came from. Surely one of the most beautiful methods in differential equations could not have originated as a fortunate guess. Somewhere in history, someone had to look at a differential equation for the very first time and ask, *What kind of function should I try?* That historical moment fascinated me far more than the computational procedure itself.

As I reflected on this question, I began to imagine the mathematicians who first confronted these problems centuries ago. They had no symbolic algebra software, no numerical solvers, and no modern computers to guide their intuition. They possessed only paper, ink, patience, and an extraordinary ability to observe patterns hidden beneath complexity. Rather than asking how to manipulate an equation mechanically, they must first have tried to understand the very nature of differentiation itself. Differentiation is an operation that measures change. Every time we differentiate a function, we ask how it evolves. If one hopes to solve a differential equation, perhaps the first question should not be about the equation at all, but about the kinds of functions that behave naturally under differentiation.

This perspective completely changed the way I viewed the problem. Consider a polynomial. Each differentiation reduces its degree until eventually nothing remains. A trigonometric function behaves differently, but it too continually changes its identity, alternating between sine and cosine with successive derivatives. Neither family of functions remains fundamentally the same under differentiation. The exponential function, however, possesses a remarkable property. Every time it is differentiated, its essential form is preserved. The derivative is simply a constant multiple of the original function. Differentiation changes its magnitude, but it never changes its identity. What initially appeared to be a clever computational trick now revealed itself as something much deeper: mathematicians had discovered a function that naturally cooperates with the operation defining the problem.

The longer I reflected upon this idea, the more I realized that it extends far beyond differential equations. In many ways, life itself resembles differentiation. Every experience changes us. Success changes us, failure changes us, responsibility changes us, and even the passage of time quietly reshapes our perspective. Change is unavoidable. The more profound question is whether we preserve our essential identity while passing through those changes. Many people alter their principles whenever circumstances become difficult. Their confidence depends upon success, their kindness depends upon convenience, and their determination depends upon favorable conditions. They change their character whenever life changes around them.

The exponential function offers a strikingly different philosophy. It changes continuously, yet it never ceases to be itself. Its scale may increase or decrease, but its identity remains intact. Perhaps genuine personal growth follows the same principle. True maturity is not the absence of change; rather, it is the ability to grow without abandoning one's deepest values. Knowledge should increase, wisdom should deepen, and experience should broaden our understanding of the world, but integrity, curiosity, compassion, humility, and discipline should remain the constants that define who we are. In that sense, the exponential function becomes far more than a mathematical object. It becomes a metaphor for resilience.

As I walked out of the classroom that afternoon, I realized that I had learned something much more valuable than a method for solving differential equations. Hidden within the characteristic equation was a timeless lesson about science, research, and life itself. The greatest breakthroughs do not always come from fighting complexity directly. More often, they begin by identifying what remains unchanged within the complexity. That insight transformed the way I now look at mathematics, and perhaps more importantly, it transformed the way I think about growth itself. The strongest mind is not one that never changes; it is one that continues to learn, adapt, and evolve while remaining faithful to the principles that define its identity.
